# V2 Sexology Research Foundation

This note is the research base for a more original V2 instrument. The goal is not to make a clinical diagnosis tool. The goal is an entertainment/personality-style quiz that is more defensible: it should measure broad erotic preference patterns, separate fantasy from real-life comfort, avoid pathologizing kink, and be clear about consent and boundaries.

## Working Thesis

V2 should move away from "DSKB as copied taxonomy" and toward an original erotic-preference profile. The strongest product idea is:

- measure multiple validated-ish constructs from sexology and relationship science;
- collapse them into a simple public type only after scoring;
- keep a richer private report underneath;
- treat fantasies as data about arousal patterns, not as literal intent;
- avoid labeling common fantasies or kink interests as deviant.

## Evidence Base

### 1. Sexual arousal is better modeled as excitation plus inhibition

The Dual Control Model argues that sexual response depends on both excitatory and inhibitory systems. Janssen, Bancroft, and colleagues operationalized this in SIS/SES, and later SESII-W/M broadened the measurement across men and women.

Useful constructs:

- excitation/arousability
- inhibition from performance worries
- inhibition from possible consequences
- setting/context effects
- partner characteristics and dyadic interaction

Why this matters for V2: "What turns you on?" is only half the instrument. We also need "what shuts the system down?" and "under what conditions does fantasy become uncomfortable in practice?"

Sources:

- Janssen, Vorst, Finn, & Bancroft (2002), SIS/SES development.
- Milhausen et al. (2010), SESII-W/M validation: six subscales including Inhibitory Cognitions, Relationship Importance, Arousability, Partner Characteristics and Behaviors, Setting, and Dyadic Elements.

### 2. Sociosexuality is not one thing

SOI-R divides unrestricted sociosexuality into three facets:

- behavior
- attitude
- desire

Penke and Asendorpf found the three-factor model fit much better than one-factor or two-factor models in a large sample. Their model is also useful because it separates what someone has done, what they believe is acceptable, and what they desire.

Why this matters for V2: we should avoid asking only direct behavior questions. A person may have high fantasy/desire but low behavior due to relationship status, culture, opportunity, safety, or personal ethics. The product should distinguish these.

Source:

- Penke & Asendorpf (2008), SOI-R.

### 3. Fantasy content should not be treated as pathology by default

Joyal, Cossette, and Lapierre surveyed 1,516 adults on 55 sexual fantasies. Their main finding is very important for this product: only two fantasies were statistically rare for women or men, thirty were common for one or both genders, and submission/domination themes were common and related.

Why this matters for V2: the app should not say "your fetish means you are broken." It should profile patterns: sensory, scenario, agency, novelty, exclusivity, intensity, consent/boundary needs, and fantasy-practice distance.

Source:

- Joyal, Cossette, & Lapierre (2015), "What Exactly Is an Unusual Sexual Fantasy?"

### 4. Kink/BDSM interests can be common and non-pathological

BDSM-related interests vary widely and include power exchange, roleplay, restraint, sensation, ritual, and play. Wismeijer and van Assen compared 902 BDSM practitioners with 434 controls and concluded BDSM may be better understood as recreational leisure rather than psychopathology. A later scoping review notes that one nationally representative study found 68.8% of participants reported at least one BDSM fantasy or practice.

Why this matters for V2: kink/fetish modules can exist, but they should feed higher-order dimensions. They should not become shame labels.

Sources:

- Wismeijer & van Assen (2013), psychological characteristics of BDSM practitioners.
- Brown, Barker, & Rahman (2020), BDSM scoping review.

### 5. Sexual satisfaction includes self, partner, and interaction domains

The New Sexual Satisfaction Scale was developed across seven samples with over 2,000 participants in Croatia and the U.S. The authors originally proposed five conceptual domains: sexual sensations, sexual awareness/focus, sexual exchange, emotional closeness, and sexual activity. Analyses suggested a two-dimensional structure: self-focused and partner/activity-focused domains.

Why this matters for V2: compatibility should not be only "same type" or "opposite type." It should consider interaction fit: whether someone's arousal pattern works with another person's preferred pacing, emotional closeness, agency, and novelty level.

Source:

- Stulhofer, Busko, & Brouillard (2010), New Sexual Satisfaction Scale.

### 6. Attachment and closeness are relevant, but should be used carefully

Adult attachment measures commonly model anxiety and avoidance as dimensions. ECR/ECR-R style thinking is useful for relationship comfort, closeness, dependency, and fear of rejection, but online quizzes often overclaim here.

Why this matters for V2: use attachment-adjacent items only as lightweight "intimacy climate" signals. Do not tell users they have a clinical attachment style.

Sources:

- Brennan, Clark, & Shaver (1998), ECR.
- ECR short-form validation literature.

### 7. Japan-specific context matters

For Japanese users, sexual communication, sexlessness, fatigue, and relationship role expectations may be more culturally salient than in many Western quiz products. JEX/JFPA-style surveys repeatedly report high levels of sexlessness among married couples. The 2024 JEX survey reports sexless-marriage figures rising substantially over time, and secondary reporting around JFPA surveys has cited about 48.3% of respondents as having sex zero times per month under that survey definition.

Why this matters for V2: Japanese copy should be less "declare your fetish identity" and more "understand your desire pattern, comfort conditions, and relational mismatch." This also gives the quiz more social usefulness.

Sources:

- JEX Japan Sex Survey 2024.
- Japan Family Planning Association reporting via Japanese media.

## Proposed V2 Measurement Model

Instead of DSKB, V2 should measure 8-10 subscales and then produce a public type. Draft subscales:

1. Fantasy-practice distance
   - Do fantasies stay as fantasies, or does the user want real-world enactment?

2. Excitation / arousability
   - How easily does desire activate from cues, thoughts, people, images, or situations?

3. Inhibition / safety brakes
   - What shuts desire down: performance worry, consequences, exposure, disgust, pain, relationship insecurity, moral discomfort?

4. Sensory focus
   - Body, touch, visual details, sensation, physical immediacy.

5. Scenario focus
   - Story, role, taboo frame, ritual, language, status, psychological context.

6. Agency orientation
   - Lead/direct/initiate vs yield/follow/be guided; include "switch/context-dependent" as a real possibility.

7. Relational breadth
   - Exclusive/depth-oriented vs novelty/open/socially exploratory.

8. Emotional closeness requirement
   - Desire needs trust/bonding vs desire can be playful, casual, or low-attachment.

9. Intensity and novelty tolerance
   - Preference for soft/familiar vs unusual/risky-feeling/experimental stimuli.

10. Communication/assertiveness comfort
   - Ability to name wants, refuse, negotiate, and recover from mismatch.

## Question Design Rules

- Hide subscale labels during the quiz.
- Separate fantasy appeal from real-life comfort.
- Avoid explicit illegal/non-consensual endorsement items. If discussing fantasy themes, frame consent and boundaries clearly.
- Ask about patterns and reactions, not identity labels.
- Use indirect items where possible: frequency of thoughts, comfort in imagined scenarios, shutdown conditions, communication preferences.
- Include reverse-keyed items, but do not make them tricky or shaming.
- Use 5-point Likert for mobile speed unless psychometric validation later suggests otherwise.

## Minimum V2 Instrument Shape

Pilot version:

- 48 items total
- 6 items per major public axis if we choose 4 public axes
- 4-6 extra validity/consistency items
- Japanese-first wording
- English as secondary
- result includes public code, subscale radar, fantasy-practice gap, safety/brake profile, and compatibility notes

Research-grade later version:

- collect anonymous responses;
- run exploratory factor analysis;
- remove weak/cross-loading items;
- test internal reliability for each subscale;
- use percentiles from the app's own data instead of arbitrary cutoffs;
- publish a transparent "how this was built" page.

## Product Direction

The public type should be original, but not random English codenames. For Japan, the best public identity may be:

- a short Japanese phrase or archetype name;
- plus a compact roman code only if each letter has a memorable concept;
- plus a character/card visual for recall.

The scientific layer should say:

"This is an entertainment quiz informed by sexology and relationship research. It is not a diagnosis, not a clinical measure, and not a substitute for professional advice."

## Open Questions

- Should V2 keep any DSKB compatibility with the current app, or fully separate?
- Do we want type codes to be alphabetic, Japanese phrase-based, or visual-card-first?
- How explicit can the questions be while still being safe, shareable, and app-store/social-platform friendly?
- Should the result split "fantasy self" and "practice self" into two cards?
- Should we design for solo users first, or eventually add partner comparison?
